## Abstract

AI coding assistants and agents raise the rate at which code is produced faster than human review
capacity grows. Evidence indicates that the code is riskier per unit than human-written code and, in
practice, less inspected. This review asks how organizations practice and scale human oversight of
AI-generated code so that oversight keeps pace with volume without sacrificing quality, including
the governance landscape and the strengths and limitations of current practice. Following PRISMA
2020, we searched four peer-reviewed databases, two preprint servers, three curated streams, and a
backward citation snowball, identifying 9,502 unique records (2020–2026). Two title-and-abstract
screens, an abstract-level relevance triage with a human review floor, and a full-text read reduced
these to 147 abstract-level Core studies, of which 72 survived the full-text read as the included
corpus; a further 961 in-scope studies were retained at abstract level as Context. The included
studies were coded against a versioned controlled vocabulary (44 frozen tags; 17 themes and 27
facets) by a three-vendor panel of language models with human adjudication of every tag that
entered synthesis; the human rejected 9.4% of panel-modal proposals, and the panel recalled 91.6% of
blind human tags while flagging only 57.4% of the studies the human later demoted. The corpus is
solution-heavy and evidence-light: 52 of 72 studies propose or build an oversight mechanism, 31 are
built systems, six report adoption outside a research setting, and 15 exist only as designs. The
problem side establishes an oversight-scaling inversion—AI code is riskier yet less inspected—and
six distinct mechanisms by which oversight fails under volume: skipped review, misallocated
attention, substitution of senior building capacity, intake restriction, reviewability decay, and
engagement decay. The solution side organizes onto a Detect → Triage → Fix → Escalate pipeline, with
detection heavily populated and triage, remediation gating, and outcome measurement thin. Three
structural results recur across independent studies: a model's assessment of its own output is not a
check; agreement between models is not validation; and supplying an external specification is
necessary but produces a second failure mode, overcorrection. Only one class of mechanism—routing
that lets some artifacts skip human review—reduces human load; the rest reallocate a fixed budget.
The literature is rich in deciding where to look and poor in measuring whether looking works. These
gaps motivate the dissertation's organizational survey.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The oversight-scaling inversion

Generative AI for software engineering entered the mainstream with GitHub Copilot in 2021 and has
since expanded from an assistant that completes lines of code into agents that plan, write, test,
and open pull requests across the development lifecycle. The volume of code produced per engineer
has risen accordingly. What has not risen in step is the capacity to inspect it. In conventional
engineering practice, riskier artifacts attract more scrutiny. For AI-generated code the two curves
cross: the code is measurably riskier per unit, and it receives less inspection.

Both halves are now documented. On risk: in real GitHub projects, 29.5% of Python and 24.2% of
JavaScript snippets generated by Copilot and comparable tools carry security weaknesses spanning
43 CWE categories (Fu et al., 2025 [U3IQJ4VK]). Across 302.6k verified AI-authored commits in 6,299
repositories, AI-introduced code smells, correctness issues, and security issues are tracked to the
latest revision, and 22.7% persist (Liu et al., 2026 [9H6FWJME]). A difference-in-differences
study of Cursor adoption finds a large but transient velocity gain alongside a persistent increase
in static-analysis warnings (+30%) and code complexity (+41%), with the latter driving the
long-term slowdown (He et al., 2026 [REZGA5WF]). A matched-control audit finds 1.80× the rate of
high-severity findings in AI-attributed files, with the failures preserving the appearance of
functionality (Parris, 2026 [3SU9QZ6F]).

On inspection: among AI-co-authored pull requests from contributors without prior code ownership,
approximately 80% were merged without any explicit review—the reverse of the open-source norm, under
which non-owner contributions receive the *most* feedback—and 86.9% of the repositories had no
guidelines for AI-agent use at all (Gao et al., 2026 [59KP8GTP]). Repositories tend to auto-merge
either all or none of their agentic pull requests, and maintainers auto-merge agentic PRs more often
than human-authored ones (Branco et al., 2026 [JQPPKSFQ]). Where review does hold, someone pays:
after Copilot's introduction, core open-source developers reviewed 6.5% more code and produced 19%
less of their own (Xu et al., 2025 [F2C2DWSI]). Developers describe the resulting burden as an
"endless stream of AI slop" that externalizes the cost of individual productivity onto reviewers and
maintainers (Baltes et al., 2026 [B644HQFS]).

We call this divergence the **oversight-scaling inversion**: risk per artifact up, scrutiny per
artifact down. It is the phenomenon this review documents, and it is the opening argument of the
dissertation of which this review is the first part.

### 1.2 "Vibe coding" and the governance gap

Karpathy's (2025 [Z4IKFZJ4]) coinage *vibe coding*—describing software, accepting what the model
produces, and iterating in natural language—names the extreme of a spectrum. At one end, a human
authors code and accepts suggestions piece by piece (the *assistive* setting). At the other, an
agent plans and executes multi-step work and delivers an artifact or pull request for review (the
*agentic* setting). The oversight problem differs qualitatively between them. In the assistive
setting the oversight surface is the acceptance moment, and the characteristic failure is
over-reliance: participants with an AI assistant wrote significantly less secure code than those
without, while believing it more secure (Perry et al., 2023 [YBHHYR4P]). In the agentic setting the
surface is the gate—review, approval, merge—and the characteristic failure is the inversion above.

Organizations have no established governance for this production mode. Review obligations, audit
trails, and regulatory hooks (the EU AI Act's Article 14 on human oversight, the NIST AI Risk
Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001) are either nascent or transplanted from general-AI governance
that was not written with code in mind. Most of the organizational responses that *have* been
observed come from open-source communities, which can refuse contributions outright; employed
reviewers facing their own team's output usually cannot.

### 1.3 Review question and sub-questions

> **RQ.** How do organizations practice and scale human oversight of AI-generated code so that
> oversight keeps pace with the volume of AI-produced code without sacrificing quality—including the
> governance/policy landscape and the strengths and limitations of current oversight practices?

The question is organized by a two-part analytical frame: **quantify the problem** (including the
*insufficiency layer*—evidence that current oversight does not close the gap) and **characterize
the solution** (mechanisms, frameworks, and measurements, organized on a Detect → Triage → Fix →
Escalate pipeline derived bottom-up from the corpus). Six sub-questions structure the synthesis:

| SQ | Question | Layer |
|---|---|---|
| SQ1 | What is the nature and magnitude of the oversight gap AI-generated code creates? | Problem |
| SQ2 | How and why is current oversight insufficient to close it? | Problem |
| SQ3 | What oversight mechanisms and designs exist? | Solution — what |
| SQ4 | How is oversight enacted in practice (process, who, when, division of labour)? | Solution — how |
| SQ5 | How does oversight scale with volume without sacrificing quality? | Solution — scaling |
| SQ6 | How is oversight governed, audited, and evidenced? | Assurance |

The sub-questions were refined bottom-up from corpus structure after extraction and before
synthesis, and record descriptive structure rather than findings-claims (see §3.11 on this
guardrail).

## 2. Background and Terminology

### 2.1 Oversight, steering, and guidance

The single most consequential definitional decision in this review was how to classify a human's
act when they are "in the loop" with an AI coding tool. Three acts are distinguished:

- **Oversight** — inspection and gating of a *produced artifact* on quality or risk grounds, with
  the authority to reject or escalate. Only this earns a place on the review's solution axis.
- **Steering** — directing *generation*: prompting, specifying, fine-tuning, controlling the
  model's inputs or context. Steering happens before there is an artifact to judge.
- **Guidance** — the agent asking the human how to proceed or to unblock it.

The distinction cuts on the solution side too. A contribution that improves generation (better
prompts, executable specifications fed to the model, fine-tuning against hallucination) is steering,
and does not count as oversight however much it improves quality. A plan-approval gate is oversight
when it is a designed checkpoint with authority to block in a lifecycle with checks and balances,
and steering when it is an approval inside a conversational guide-then-go flow. This boundary was
applied at screening (Phase D) and again at coding (the `steering` facet marks where a solution
component was excluded from the solution themes for this reason; 17 of the 72 included studies
carry it).

### 2.2 Two meanings of "scalable oversight"

The phrase "scalable oversight" is a false friend. In the AI-alignment literature it denotes
**quality scaling**—helping a human evaluate model output more *accurately*, typically so that the
evaluation can train a better model (Bowman et al., 2022 [RNDPW7VA]; McAleese et al., 2024
[NRVQT89E]). The human never leaves any sample, because in a labelling regime the human's judgement
is the product. This review's premise is **throughput scaling**—more code overseen per human-hour,
by deciding what needs human eyes at all. The diagnostic that separates a paper's contribution is
whether it measures *time or volume* or only *accuracy*. A method that makes a reviewer more
correct but no faster does not scale throughput, and is reported here as a quality-scaling result.

### 2.3 Generation modes, corpus tiers, and tag layers

- **Assistive vs agentic** generation is defined by *who initiates* and *the reviewable unit*, not
  by where the tool lives; a human-initiated chat task that returns a complete artifact is treated
  as agentic because the reviewable unit dominates. Forty-six of the 72 included studies concern
  the agentic setting, 18 the assistive, eight both, and 16 neither (the AI sits only on the review
  side).
- **Core vs Context** is a scope judgement about extraction depth, not a judgement of importance.
  Core studies are *directly* about scalable human oversight of AI-generated code in an
  organizational or pipeline context **and** contribute an operationalizable mechanism,
  measurement, framework, or empirical finding. Context studies are in scope but adjacent or
  transferable—behavioural, theoretical, regulatory, or secondary—and the synthesis draws on them
  where they supply what Core cannot.
- **Themes, facets, and flags** are three orthogonal tag layers: a theme is a membership in one of
  the review's arguments (membership, not mention); a facet records a functional role, form,
  maturity, scope, mode, method, or risk type; a flag is a disposition signal. Each study carries
  exactly one *primary* theme—the argument it would be written up under in depth.

### 2.4 Adjacent literatures

**Human factors of automation.** Parasuraman and Manzey's (2010 [ING3D89M]) attentional account of
complacency and bias is the dissertation's theoretical lens and the corpus's most-cited theory;
Langer et al. (2025 [5DCQDB4C]) import signal detection theory and give the oversight literature a
vocabulary it otherwise lacks—a reviewer's *sensitivity* (does she detect the defect when she
looks?) separated from her *response criterion* (how readily does she accept?). Both are Context
here because their object is general AI, not code; both are used throughout the synthesis as
framing.

**General scalable oversight and alignment.** Transfers as theory and as the quality-scaling
contrast of §2.2; kept at Context.

**Code review research.** Mechanisms developed for human-written code—reviewer recommendation,
risk scoring, review-effort prediction—transfer to AI code and are kept distinguishable with a
`general-code` scope flag (11 of 72 included studies) so that the synthesis does not present a
mechanism *imported from* general review as one *built for* AI code.

**AI-code quality and security measurement.** Capability benchmarks and leaderboards are excluded
unless they carry a governance-relevant measurement; the measurement studies that remain are the
problem-side anchors of §4.3.

**Regulation.** The EU AI Act's Article 14, the NIST AI RMF, and ISO/IEC 42001 appear in the corpus
both as motivation and, in a minority of papers, as subject. The review distinguishes the two
(§4.8): a paper that *operationalizes* a regulatory obligation into engineering controls is
Core; one that cites the Act as motivation is not tagged for it.

**Prior secondary literature.** The corpus contains reviews of LLM code generation (e.g., Dong et
al., 2025 [D3ZU22JC]) and of AI-driven software engineering practice (Alenezi & Akour, 2025
[UG5D8G6U]), both retained as Context. No prior review was found that addresses the
oversight-*scaling* question specifically **[verify at write-up against the `lit-review` set]**.
That is the gap this review fills.

### 2.5 Theoretical framing of the method

Two engineering-management ideas anchor the method as well as the subject. Cleanroom statistical
quality control substitutes statistical sampling for exhaustive inspection; lean manufacturing's
*jidoka* has automated detection stop the line and escalate to a human only on a detected defect
**[verify citations: Cobb & Mills 1990; Ohno 1988]**. Their synthesis—automated detection routing
human attention by risk—is both the hypothesis the corpus is read against and the design of the
review's own coding pipeline (§3.9–§3.10).

## 3. Method

> **Framing note.** The review's method deliberately instantiates its own subject. Language models
> do the volume work (screening, coding); the human holds every judgement gate; every model output
> passes through designed, computed—never self-reported—quality controls; and escalation is
> risk-tiered. Section 3.10 states this plainly as *methodological alignment*, not as proof of the
> thesis, and reports the places where the pipeline exhibited the failure modes the corpus
> describes.

### 3.1 Design overview

The review follows PRISMA 2020 (Page et al., 2021). Two identification streams—database and
curated queries, and a backward citation snowball—were run through an identical screening
instrument and merged only at the final triage. The pipeline is a multi-stage funnel:

| PRISMA stage | Internal phase | Instrument | Human role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identification | Phase A | logged queries; snowball | query design; curated streams |
| Screening | Phase B (Pass 1, recall) · Phase C (Pass 2, operationalizability) | title + abstract; model screen with human override | pilot calibration; override; gating checks |
| Eligibility (a) | Phase D (relevance triage) + Phase E (human confirmation) | abstract-level core/context/discard + centrality 0–100 | reviews every record ≥ 55; confirms every Core |
| Eligibility (b) | Phases 4–5 (full-text read + coding) | full text | reads; adjudicates tier and tags |
| Included | Phase 6 | — | — |

Internal Phases 1–3 map to PRISMA Identification, Screening, and the first pass of Eligibility.
Phases 4–6 are **not** post-inclusion activity: they *are* the eligibility determination, executed
as a multi-pass process because the corpus volume makes a single abstract-level pass too coarse to
trust as final. The abstract-level triage is a provisional call; the full-text read finalizes it.
Reading each Core paper once and performing both eligibility finalization and data extraction
(thematic coding) in the same read was a deliberate volume-driven efficiency, not a conflation of
two PRISMA items.

The corpus is **two-tier**. Core studies receive full-text fetch and full coding; Context studies
are retained at abstract level and feed framing and the dissertation's survey design. Tier is a
scope judgement, not a quality judgement, and the two are recorded on separate axes.

**System of record.** A Zotero group library (6505702) holds every record. Lineage is encoded as
namespaced tags (`source:*`, `s1:*`, `s2:*`, `s3:*`, `centrality:*`, `cocite:*`; later
`demote:*`, `cal:*`, `final:*`). Every stage is scripted, resumable, and auditable; the scripts,
prompts, decision log, and per-run model outputs are in a public repository, with copyrighted full
texts excluded. **Screening decisions are a layered history, never a mutable state:** a human
decision that overrides a model's is recorded alongside it, never over it, so inter-rater data are
preserved and the final state is always recomputable from the record.

### 3.2 Search strategy (Phase A)

**Sources.** Four peer-reviewed databases (IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Scopus, Web of
Science); two preprint servers (arXiv, SSRN), included because a large share of work on a
phenomenon this new has not yet reached peer review; and three curated streams declared in advance
and capped—*Coursework* (the author's prior reading, self-assembled rather than committee-curated),
*Practitioner Network* (a prospectively declared list of monitored practitioner sources), and
*Committee Recommendations*. ABI/INFORM was evaluated and rejected after a calibration query returned
3 hits against Scopus's 110 for the same query.

**Queries.** Searches were organized in six thematic categories—AI code risk and security; AI code
quality and debt; human oversight and capacity; organizational governance and policy;
organizational risk recognition; and tool-specific—as OR-grouped synonym clusters AND-ed across
categories, favouring recall over precision at the search stage. *Vibe coding* was treated as an
exact phrase; broader constructs were not phrase-locked. One row per query (identifier, string,
database, field, date, hit count) is kept in the query log **[verify: `Query_Composition_and_Log.xlsx`
is outside the repository; raw pre-dedupe hit counts come from it]**. Date range: 2020–2026, with
a secondary 2018–2019 pass permitted for foundational theory only. **No full-text-availability
filter** was applied at search time, since that would make the corpus a function of institutional
licences; non-retrievable items were to be coded `not-retrievable` at full-text screening.

**Saturation.** Web of Science, the last database added, yielded 116 unique records (35%) against
65% overlap with IEEE, ACM, Scopus, and arXiv, which was taken as evidence that the multi-database
strategy had reached reasonable saturation. Synthesis gaps found later were addressed by targeted
back-fill queries in existing databases rather than by adding sources.

**Grey literature.** Web, vendor, and social content was generally excluded, retained only as a
small number of hand-flagged exceptions of extreme relevance (Karpathy's coinage of the term is one).
**Preprints are not grey literature** in this review: they are treated as ordinary literature and
make up 32 of the 72 included studies. The justification is recency rather than relaxed standards:
41 of the 72 included studies are dated 2026 and 22 are dated 2025, and the preprint share rises
toward the present—the signature of publication lag on a fast-moving topic. Excluding preprints would
have preferentially removed the newest work. Preprints that are subsequently published are updated
in place, with the original type and date preserved as lineage tags.

**AI assistance in search.** A language model was used to suggest candidate sources; every suggestion
was treated as a lead requiring independent verification under the same criteria, and no AI-suggested
citation entered the corpus without being verified against the original.

### 3.3 Title-and-abstract screening (Phases B–C)

**Phase B — recall screen (Pass 1).** Default to keep; discard only on clear off-scope. A record is
kept if its abstract hits a direct tier (vibe coding or AI code generation with a governance, risk,
or oversight angle; code-review capacity or scalability; AI code risk and quality; organizational
governance of AI coding tools; developer oversight of LLM-generated code) or a supporting tier
(human-oversight frameworks; AI governance with a software or DevSecOps angle; scalable oversight of
high-volume AI output). *Maybe* was reserved for abstracts too thin to decide. Two always-on rules:
documents authored by the reviewer are discarded; course-platform pages are discarded.

**Phase C — operationalizability screen (Pass 2).** Applied to Phase-B keeps and maybes, with the
test: *does this paper give a software-engineering organization something it can act on—a
measurement, metric, mechanism, detection signal, framework, or process?* Papers that only establish
that a problem exists were discarded. Seven recurring false positives were excluded by rule:
descriptive-only risk; governance applied only to non-software domains; non-software
human-in-the-loop; AI ethics without an operational mechanism; creative-content copyright without a
code angle; agentic infrastructure with no control mechanism; and adoption studies concerned only
with productivity or return on investment.

**Abstract gate.** Title-only screening is unreliable, so records still lacking an abstract after a
deliberate metadata-fetch effort were *held*—neither auto-screened nor auto-discarded—and filed for
human exception review (121 snowball records; none advanced).

**Model roles and their reliability.** Claude models (Sonnet, then Opus as arbiter) performed the
production screens; ChatGPT and Gemini screened samples for cross-model comparison; the human
screened a calibration sample and overrode source by source. In a pilot in which all four raters
screened the same 100 SSRN records, the human and Claude each kept 32%, ChatGPT 68%, and Gemini 59%.
Agreement with the human was fair for Claude (κ = 0.273) and slight for ChatGPT (0.076) and Gemini
(0.052), so Claude was adopted as the production screening model with the human as final arbiter.
The ordering held at every stage: the human is the most conservative rater, Claude sits closest to
the human, and ChatGPT and Gemini keep roughly twice as much (pooled Pass 1: human 19.6%, Claude
29.8%, ChatGPT 56.1%, Gemini 64.7%; the human figure is source-confounded and is quoted as "about
20%"). In a 600-record cross-model batch, Gemini returned hallucinated record identifiers for more
than half, leaving 259 usable decisions.

Before Pass-2 decisions were applied, an unblinded **Trust Check** on a stratified 60-record sample
(20 keep / 20 maybe / 20 discard) gave κ = 0.792 between the Sonnet→Opus pipeline and the human
(n = 58); this is a gating statistic, not the citable reliability figure. A blinded 100-record
validation sample was drawn (seed 42) but **never coded**, so the citable blinded Pass-2 κ remains
open (§6).

### 3.4 Backward citation snowball

References co-cited by the triaged Core set were imported (2,787 records), banded by co-citation
count, enriched (OpenAlex back-fill, title canonicalization, cross-type consolidation), and
de-duplicated against the query stream under a one-record-per-study rule (the journal version is
the keeper for preprint–journal pairs; the query-stream record is master). The snowball stream was
then screened with the **identical** Phase B → C → D instrument, gated by the same abstract gate,
and reviewed at the same human floor, so that both streams met the same bar before merging. Fifty-two
snowball hits were already in the query stream and were counted there.

### 3.5 Relevance triage and human confirmation (Phases D–E)

**Phase D.** Opus assigned each eligible record a bin (core / context / discard) and a centrality
score 0–100, author-blind to avoid prestige bias. **Core (70–100) requires both** that the paper be
directly about scalable human oversight or governance of AI-generated code in an organizational or
pipeline context **and** that it contribute an operationalizable mechanism, measurement, framework,
or empirical finding. Context (30–69) is in scope but adjacent and transferable, with an evidence
base or concrete framework; secondary literature defaults here. Discard (0–29) is weak and
off-core, with a single reason code (out-of-scope · wrong-level · wrong-type · too-old ·
quality-threshold-not-met).

Operational discriminators refined during review tightened the bar: effectiveness-of-oversight
studies are candidate Core, tool-capability benchmarks are Context, and bare leaderboards are
Context or discard; agent self-conformance ("make the agent follow best practices") is out of
Core; framing and definitional sources are kept tight, with one empirically grounded anchor at Core
and the rest at Context. The oversight/steering/guidance discriminator (§2.1) was decisive at the
boundary.

**Phase E — human confirmation.** Cross-model QA on a stratified 250-record sample showed that Opus
over-calls Core, that errors are two-sided, and—most consequentially—that the other models' dissent
from Opus does not predict the human's judgement. All three models classified more records as Core
than the human (Opus 20%, GPT-5.5 38%, Gemini 30%, human 16% on the 50-record human-coded subset);
agreement with the human was fair for Opus (binary keep/discard κ = 0.297) and at chance for
GPT-5.5 (0.041) and Gemini (0.010), while the models agreed with one another at κ 0.49–0.64. Of the
eight records Opus kept but the human discarded, neither other model flagged any. Agreement among
models therefore reflected shared tendencies rather than correctness, and **no model shortcut around
human review was available**.

Consequently the **human reviewed every record with centrality ≥ 55**, in exact parity across
streams, in four bands: Confirm (Core ≥ 75), Review (Core 70–74, the demote-risk cliff), Recall
(Context 55–69, a hunt for hidden Cores), and Below-55 (auto-dispositioned, not reviewed). Every
Core is human-confirmed. Against this full review, final Core was 60% of Opus's Core calls in the
query stream and 57% in the snowball stream (the n = 50 sample estimate of ~30% was a small-sample
low). In total the human made 1,901 coding decisions used in reliability statistics and 428
Core-confirmation decisions.

### 3.6 Full-text acquisition and eligibility finalization

PDFs were fetched for confirmed Cores only, converted to text, and attached beside the PDF in Zotero
so that full-text reads are reproducible from a stored artifact rather than an ad-hoc conversion
(0 extraction failures). Full texts live only in Zotero and local caches, never in the public
repository. The full-text read finalized each Core study's tier: **75 of 147 (51.0%) were demoted
to Context**, every reversal running one way (Core → Context; no full read promoted a Context
paper into the included set). The demotion rate is itself reported as a measure of abstract-only
screening reliability (§4.1).

### 3.7 The thematic instrument

**Derivation.** The controlled vocabulary was derived bottom-up by open-coding the Core full texts
(four parallel coders working from an identical frame, merged into emergent clusters) and
cross-checked against the author's practitioner experience as a comprehension aid. It organizes
solution themes on a **Detect → Triage → Fix → Escalate** spine with problem, limits, governance,
and supply-chain families beside it; each theme definition states what it captures, where its
boundary lies, and worked examples. The vocabulary was co-authored by the human and Claude, then
refined by the human; it was not produced by the models that later applied it.

**Layers.** Three orthogonal layers (§2.3): `theme:` (membership, not mention), facets (role, form
and maturity, scope, mode, contribution, risk type, method—a seven-question checklist per paper),
and flags (demote; insufficient input). One **primary** theme per paper, chosen by where the paper's
distinctive contribution and main effort live (the altitude rule), not by its standard scaffolding.

**Change control.** The instrument was **frozen** at v2.13 (44 tags: 17 themes + 27 facets) for
gauge constancy before the production coding pass. Refinements discovered during use were queued in
the changelog and never applied mid-measurement; the instrument of record is a verbatim prompt
capture (`Tag_Prompt_v2.13.md`), physically separate from the arbiter's living cheat-sheet. Every
one of the cheat-sheet's tags fired at least once (no dead vocabulary). After the pass closed, a
versioned cut (F2) added two tags and re-specified three existing constructs as new `-v2`/`-v3`
slugs **without editing the original definitions**, so that all versions coexist in the record and a
governing-version map states which one the synthesis reads. The vocabulary grew from 44 frozen to
51 at sweep close and 56 reportable slugs after F2.

### 3.8 Calibration

The coding approach was developed and checked in three calibration rounds before broad tagging
began. In the first, ten randomly selected Core studies (seed 714) were co-tagged by the human and
Claude. This round served to validate the tagging: the gaps in the vocabulary and the problems with
tag definitions that it exposed were identified and resolved before the next round. In the second,
the human tagged a further ten randomly selected studies first, without seeing any model output.
This human-labelled set served as a training set: the coding instructions were refined against it
(v2.2 → v2.13), and the models' tags were scored against it. In the third round, a single study was
used to check the AI-first workflow: the AI models tagged first under the frozen instructions, and
the human validated their tags. Broad tagging of the corpus then began in that mode, with the human
adjudicating the model proposals on every study retained for synthesis. No model was trained or
fine-tuned; "training" here means only that the coding instructions and vocabulary were refined
against human labels.

Two calibration results carried forward. First, the blind round provides the only unassisted
human arm: the panel collectively proposed **91.6%** (76 of 83) of the tags the blind human assigned
(89.4% on the co-tagged set). Second, human origination of tags did not differ between the
model-first and blind arms (10.4% vs 7.8%), so **no anchoring of the human to model proposals was
detected**. Negative design results were also recorded: persona framing of the tagger prompt was
rejected for drift toward salience; splitting the theme and facet prompts was declined; no effect
of paper length on panel agreement was found (r ≈ 0.0, n = 20).

### 3.9 Production coding: panel, replication, and human adjudication

**Panel.** Three vendors at top reasoning tier—Claude Opus (`claude-opus-4-8`), OpenAI
(`gpt-5.6-sol` via `codex`), and Gemini 3.1 Pro—each read the **full text** of each study and
returned strict JSON: one primary theme, all theme memberships, facets, and a ≤12-word rationale per
tag (a tag without a rationale was discarded). Vendor diversity is deliberate decorrelation. Taggers
never saw one another's output; **no model adjudicated another model**; and the panel was not run
to reach consensus—disagreement is the measurement. The approach extends Choudhuri et al. (2026
[ID7IN65K]), who used independent coding by three models with human codebook approval; here the
human reviewed every proposed tag, not only the codebook.

**Replication and tripwires.** Each model ran each paper up to three times, so that intra-model
instability could be measured separately from inter-model disagreement. Papers with any
disagreement entered a replication stage (282 additional runs on 47 contested papers), followed by
computed tripwires: tag sprawl, demote flags, and **within-model instability**—the operationalized
low-confidence signal, computed rather than self-reported. Replication resolved 41 of 47 contested
papers without human load. Instability at sweep scale was panel-uniform (17–18 tripwires per
model), indicating that run-to-run noise is a property of the task rather than a defect of one
vendor.

**The abandoned confidence gate.** The original design routed papers by panel agreement: unanimous
(3/3) papers were to be accepted automatically with only a 10% seeded audit. This was abandoned
before the production pass for two reasons, and the change is reported as a correction rather than a
refinement. In principle, gating review on panel agreement lets the thing being checked decide
whether it needs checking—the producer's own signal, precisely what the corpus shows practitioners
doing when they delegate review to the generator (§4.4). In practice, unanimous error was measured
on the calibration set: the panel agreed 9/9 on a `regulatory-compliance` tag the human rejected
(the Act was motivation, not contribution), 9/9 on a `rules-based-checks` tag for what was a
benchmarking oracle rather than a deployable check, 9/9 on a demote the human overruled, and 8/9
with the polarity of a counterpoint inverted. **Vendor decorrelation reduces correlated error but
does not eliminate shared misreadings of the instrument, and those are invisible to agreement
statistics.** Unanimity measures how legible the instrument is, not whether the tag is true.

**Human protocol as practised.** All 128 production-band Core papers received a human tier call.
Band determined the *depth* of tag verification, never *whether* a human ruled: 44 Accept, 78 Light
Read (a per-paper checklist pass), and 6 Full Read (the papers on which the panel split; all six were
demoted on full reading, as expected of the band that concentrated contested cases). The arbiter did
not tag unaided in the supervised bands: because the vocabulary exceeds working memory, the arbiter
suggested tags, the assistant validated them against the instrument and proposed omissions, and the
arbiter adjudicated everything on the table—human adjudication over machine-maintained recall, in
both directions. This is stated because it changes what the endorsement statistic means: the honest
claim is that *the arbiter, assisted, endorsed*, not that the arbiter independently agreed. The
assistant is correlated with the arbiter by construction and is not a second rater.

**Three-state human layer.** Because the arbiter's pass was non-exhaustive on early papers,
silence was given a defined meaning: on a surviving paper reviewed early, an unqueried panel-modal
tag is *implicitly confirmed*; on a demoted paper it is *unverified* (demotion short-circuits tag
verification, since the tags have no downstream consumer); a tag the panel never proposed and the
human never added is *no signal*. Later papers were confirmed or rejected tag by tag, and the early
residue was explicitly closed before synthesis. **No tag entered the final set on model output
alone.**

**Reportable tag set.** The final set is computed, not hand-assembled: *final = panel modal (≥2
distinct vendors) ∪ human endorsements − human rejections − deprecated vocabulary*. On the 72
included studies this yields **843 tags** (658 endorsed panel-modal, 113 human-only, 72 primaries;
no panel-modal tag survived on silence), a mean of 11.7 tags per study (median 12, range 5–19). On
the studies coded after calibration and retained for synthesis, the human **rejected 9.4%** of
tags on which at least two of three models agreed (48 of 509). The panel was a decent tagger and a
poor triager: against the human's tier decisions it flagged only **57.4%** of the studies the human
later demoted (tier precision 71.4%), with errors in both directions—a 9/9 demote the human
overruled, and 7 human demotes on the Accept band that carried zero model flags.

**Versioned re-runs.** After the pass, two new constructs (`deterministic-orchestration-v2`,
`rules-based-checks-v3`) and a re-specified `oversight-scaling-inversion-v2` were coded on the 72
included studies only, each behind a **calibration gate**: blind anchor papers were kept out of the
prompt, and a run failed if any required anchor was missed or any excluded anchor fired at ≥2/3.
Both gates failed on their first round (one false positive, one miss), the instrument wording was
corrected, and both passed on the second. The human then ruled on 144 per-paper tag decisions (103
endorsements, 41 rejections), all written to the library.

### 3.10 Use of AI tools, and reflexivity

Language models assisted with search-lead generation, screening, triage, text extraction, and
thematic coding, and an assistant mediated the arbiter's own coding pass. All human screening and
coding was performed by the author. At each stage where models were used, their output was compared
with a human-coded sample, and a model whose decisions departed substantially from the human's was
not used to make decisions. No study was included, and no tag assigned, on model output alone.
Premium-tier model runs were used only for targeted tie-breaks under per-run authorization.

The pipeline is an instance of the review's thesis: models produce volume, humans hold judgement
gates, quality signals are computed rather than self-reported, and escalation is risk-tiered. It is
reported here as *methodological alignment*, not as evidence. In the same spirit, three failures of
the pipeline that match the corpus's own findings are reported rather than smoothed over (§4.10): a
machine stage reversed human screening decisions without review (eight records, found and
re-adjudicated at closeout); the panel vote counter counted tags rather than vendors, so a single
model's primary passed as a two-of-three majority on 15 proposals until the defect was found and
the statistics regenerated (no human ruling was invalidated); and silence in the human layer was at
one point read as confirmation when it had three meanings. Each was caught by a deliberate audit,
not by the pipeline.

### 3.11 Threats to validity

**Construct.** The instrument moved during the blind calibration round (v2.2 → v2.13), so early
blind tags predate rules the models later saw; final adjudicated tags are consistent with the frozen
instrument, and the confound is disclosed. Calibration papers named in the instrument scored
inflated when self-referential; production papers were unnamed and unaffected. A single arbiter
made every adjudication, mitigated by binding written rulings in a public changelog (182 sections)
and by never editing a definition in place. The six sub-questions were refined bottom-up after
extraction; the refinement is documented as structural, and no findings-claims were written into
the questions.

**Internal.** Run-to-run model noise is real and decision-changing; it was characterized by
replication and modal votes rather than assumed away. Model self-report was designed out of every
routing decision. Screening-model bias was two-sided and bounded by the ≥ 55 human floor, below
which records were excluded without human review.

**External.** The field moves quickly; 63 of 72 included studies are from 2025–2026 and 32 are
preprints. Grey literature was largely excluded. Most of the problem-side evidence is repository
mining of open-source projects, whose dynamics may not transfer to organizations with employed
reviewers and stricter policies.

**Reproducibility.** Model tiers are pinned with per-run provenance records; the calibration seed
is fixed (714); scripts, prompts, changelog, and per-run outputs are public; full texts are not.

### 4.3 SQ1 — Magnitude of the problem

The problem side rests on a small set of anchors (facet `problem-statement-anchor`, 9 studies),
most of them mining studies of open-source repositories.

**Risk per artifact is elevated.** Security: 29.5% of Python and 24.2% of JavaScript AI-generated
snippets in real repositories carry CWE weaknesses (Fu et al., 2025 [U3IQJ4VK]); a controlled user
study found participants with an assistant wrote less secure code while believing it more secure
(Perry et al., 2023 [YBHHYR4P]); on security-sensitive feature tasks an agent's solutions were
functionally correct 61% of the time and secure 10.5% of the time (Zhao et al., 2025 [4PSM6ZCD],
Context). Quality: AI-introduced issues persist in 22.7% of cases to the latest revision (Liu et
al., 2026 [9H6FWJME]); Cursor adoption raises warnings 30% and complexity 41%, persistently (He et
al., 2026 [REZGA5WF]); agent-authored pull requests show more redundancy and less code reuse than
human ones (Huang et al., 2026 [4T5QFWZE]); AI-generated build code introduces maintainability and
security smells (Ghammam & Almukhtar, 2026 [SHK6KAX6]). The profile is not uniformly negative:
agentic PRs introduce *fewer* breaking changes than human-authored ones (3.45% vs 7.40%), with the
risk concentrated in maintenance tasks—refactoring 6.72% and chores 9.35%—rather than feature
generation (Ferdous et al., 2026 [UIXCRBQX]).

**Self-assessment cannot substitute for inspection.** Coding agents rated 99.9% of their PRs at 8 or
above on a 10-point confidence scale, and breaking-change rates were flat across that band—a signal
with no variance cannot triage anything (Ferdous et al., 2026 [UIXCRBQX]). Intrinsic model
confidence is poorly calibrated to code correctness (expected calibration error 0.09–0.73), and is
rescued only by fitting against an external ground truth (Spiess et al., 2025 [VTDG995V]).

**Inspection per artifact is reduced.** The anchor is Gao et al. (2026 [59KP8GTP]): ~80% of
AI-co-authored PRs from non-owners merged with no explicit review; AI PRs merge significantly faster
with the least feedback; 67.5% come from contributors with no prior ownership; 86.9% of repositories
have no AI-agent guidelines. Branco et al. (2026 [JQPPKSFQ]) find auto-merge is bimodal—repositories
merge all or none—and less common in more mature, well-governed projects. Minh et al. (2026
[74GE3TF7]) find review concentrated in a small subset of 33,707 agent PRs, with 28.3% merging
instantly and an "attention tax" of abandonment when agents meet subjective feedback.

**The causal chain.** These are one mechanism seen at several points, not independent findings: the
open gate (Gao) is the cause; persistence of issues (Liu) is the consequence of nobody gating them;
the quality tax (He) is what accumulates; and the burden falling on core developers (Xu) is who
absorbs it when review *does* happen. **Prescription and violation come from independent sources.**
He et al. conclude that AI-generated code "requir[es] extra scrutiny during review" on measured
grounds; Gao et al. measure it receiving the least. Neither cites the other. The productivity
defence does not hold either: He et al. find a 3–5× velocity gain in the first month that dissipates
after two, while the complexity cost persists—the gain is transient and the cost permanent.

### 4.4 SQ2 — How and why current oversight is insufficient

The corpus documents **six distinct mechanisms** by which oversight fails under volume. They have
different remedies, and an organization can be in any of them without knowing which.

| Mechanism | What gives way | Kind | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inversion** (skipped review) | the review itself; code ships unreviewed | structural — volume | Branco [JQPPKSFQ]; Wang [2KPHQ5IV]; Waseem [T2EG4BE2]; Pimenova [E9RAWBDT] |
| **Misallocation** | attention aimed away from the higher-risk population while reviewers are present and responding | structural — norm | Gao [59KP8GTP] |
| **Substitution** | senior *building* capacity, spent on review and rework | structural — cost | Xu [F2C2DWSI] |
| **Intake restriction** | work is refused rather than triaged (fail-closed) | structural — policy | Yang [XJAXB98T]; Ehsani [NZJST99D], Context |
| **Reviewability decay** | the artifact cannot carry what review needs | structural — representation | Wang [2KPHQ5IV]; Casserini [95CPB7CF]; Mitchell & Shaaban [6ZW9QNQH]; Maes [59ZW4R58] |
| **Engagement decay** | the reviewer's attention declines while reviewing | temporal — intra-session | Catalan [5BAZZWHG]; theory in Parasuraman & Manzey [ING3D89M] |

**Inversion and misallocation are different.** Branco's repositories skip review because they cannot
keep up. Gao's reviewers *are* reviewing—they give the least scrutiny to newcomers using AI, which
reverses a norm open source otherwise maintains. The latter is less predictable and runs against the
field's own practice; an organization would not detect it from inside, because each review decision
looks locally reasonable.

**The inversion is bimodal, not universal.** Two studies of the same population (the AIDev
agent-PR dataset) report opposite tails: auto-merged PRs ship unreviewed (fail-open; Branco), while
not-merged PRs are abandoned (fail-closed; Ehsani et al., 2026 [NZJST99D], Context). Repositories
tend to one mode or the other, and governance maturity predicts which. **The right question is not
whether the inversion happens but in which organizations and what distinguishes them**—an empirical
question about organizational characteristics that a survey can answer and a code audit cannot.

**Substitution has a price tag that aggregate metrics hide.** In Xu et al.'s panel, productivity
rises, concentrated in less-experienced contributors whose code needs rework; core developers review
6.5% more and produce 19% less. Aggregate throughput goes *up*, so an organization tracking commit
volume sees AI working as advertised while its experienced engineers stop building.

**Intake restriction is what saturated communities actually do.** Yang et al. (2026 [XJAXB98T])
analysed governance materials from 67 highly visible open-source projects and found twelve
strategies in four groups—entry admissibility and input qualification, responsibility and evidence
restoration, review-burden and workflow protection, and infrastructure and institutional
adjustment—three of which operate on the *input* side. Practitioners state the thesis themselves:
"review is the bottleneck. Not code generation" (Oh My Zsh); low-effort AI submissions are "a
Denial-of-service attack on our human effort" (FastAPI). Exclusion needs no assessment capacity,
which is why saturated projects reach for it: a community with no spare review capacity cannot
afford to triage; it can only afford to refuse. This predicts that risk-based routing appears in
organizations with slack and blanket restriction in those without—and that the pattern may not
transfer to employers, where a reviewer cannot simply refuse the team's output.

**Reviewability decay means more review time would not help.** Wang et al. (2026 [2KPHQ5IV]) argue
that the dominant artifact of AI-assisted development—code plus chat history—performs "dimension
collapse": reviewers "cannot determine what invariants were assumed, what changed, or why a
regression occurred. This is not a generation failure but a control failure." Casserini et al.
(2026 [95CPB7CF]) name the accumulating divergence between agent actions and architectural intent
*agentic entropy*, invisible to diff-based review; Mitchell and Shaaban (2025 [6ZW9QNQH]) supply a
mechanism—LLMs "prioritize user commands over code consistency," so accumulated natural-language
constraints silently contradict one another; Maes (2025 [59ZW4R58]) observes generated code
becoming unintelligible even to its own developers. Routing a reviewer to the right diff does not
help if the diff cannot carry what the reviewer needs. This is a real limit on routing as the
scaling answer (§4.7).

**Engagement decay makes oversight quality a decreasing function of time on task.** Catalan et al.
(2026 [5BAZZWHG]) find software engineers' cognitive engagement "consistently declines as tasks
progress" with an agentic assistant, and that current designs offer few affordances for reflection
or verification. The study is a four-participant formative probe; the mechanism is carried by
Parasuraman and Manzey's vigilance-decrement account, with Catalan as the software-engineering
instance. Three studies that appear to disagree—Omidvar-Tehrani et al. (2024 [4FGIVVTG]) find
reviewers recruited to review diffs hold AI to teammate standards; Catalan finds decay across
cycles; Ghammam and Almukhtar (2026 [SHK6KAX6]) find oversight theatre in artifacts in the wild—are
reconciled as points on one **scrutiny decay curve**: full attention at t = 0, a downward slope, and
theatre as the asymptote. The mechanism connecting them is the *idealized reviewer*: a participant
recruited to review is attentive by construction, so studies of that participant measure the
ceiling, not the operating point. Where a study's human arm is a paid, unhurried, single-tasked
reviewer, its human-alone baseline is optimistic and its human-plus-AI benefit is an upper bound
(McAleese et al., 2024 [NRVQT89E]).

**Oversight theatre.** A distinct limit from cognitive failure is structural: review that exists on
paper without the authority, time, or information to change the outcome. Eze (2026 [9MV2IVNU])
names *Rubber-Stamp Risk* and operationalizes it as override rate ("an extremely low OR may indicate
rubber-stamping"); Jessee (2026 [JVWUYDME]) names the human-in-the-loop who absorbs blame without
control *Scapegoat-as-a-Service* and proposes *human-in-command* governance of an enforced
operating envelope; Zhu et al. (2026 [ZGST9CY6]) distinguish *meaningful* oversight from automation
in disguise and attach signals to track (veto precision, audit reversal rate). Sterz et al. (2024
[TW4I6DU6]) supply the conditions—causal power, epistemic access, self-control, fitting
intentions—under which oversight can be effective at all. A behavioural result from outside code
gives these a mechanism: across seven experiments (N = 2,895), participants who expected their work
to be *reviewed* were *less* likely to revise AI output, because editing means owning the edits
(Zhou & Zhao, 2026 [E689ZAXC], Context). Accountability pressure converted into deference rather
than scrutiny—the opposite of what most of the solution literature assumes a sign-off gate does.

**Practice violates what the design literature prescribes.** Developers overwhelmed by review
volume "recommend delegating review back to the AI by asking it to audit its own code" (Pimenova et
al., 2025 [E9RAWBDT]). The next section shows why that is the one move the evidence forbids.

### 4.5 SQ3 — What oversight mechanisms exist

The solution themes are stages of one loop, and a single system often spans several. Each stage is
reported with its population, its best-evidenced instances, and its measured limits.

#### 4.5.1 Detect — two kinds of detector

The review separates detectors by **signal epistemics**: a *probabilistic* detector judges (an LLM
reviewer; can hallucinate) and a *deterministic* detector computes a verdict against a spec, test, or
oracle (cannot hallucinate; blind outside its spec). Twelve included studies carry both, typically
grey-zone systems such as rubric-grounded judges or agents that write and run tests.

**AI review (`ai-review`, 30 studies, 13 primary).** The population includes single LLM reviewers,
multi-agent panels, and critics. Production deployments report what makes them usable. BitsAI-CR at
ByteDance pairs a RuleChecker over 219 review rules with a ReviewFilter that validates each finding
before a developer sees it, reaching 75.0% precision and introducing an *Outdated Rate* that
measures whether developers act on comments—the filter exists because the first stage hallucinated
(Sun et al., 2025 [V4IRKSFI]). SGCR grounds review in human-authored specifications compiled into an
explicit deterministic path beside a heuristic one, and reports 42% developer adoption against a
22% baseline in a live industrial setting (Wang et al., 2025 [CTGGMIX9]). Meta's diff risk scores
gate risky changes in release deployment (Abreu et al., 2025 [BU73N7PC]). Critic models trained to
find bugs in model-written code were preferred to human critiques 63% of the time and found hundreds
of errors in data previously rated flawless—but made contractors more correct, not faster (McAleese
et al., 2024 [NRVQT89E]; a quality-scaling result, §2.2). Multi-agent review systems include
CodeAgent, with a QA-Checker supervising that agents address the review question (Tang et al., 2024
[7V7SRG43]), TheBotCompany's Strategy → Execution → Verification state machine (Lyu et al., 2026
[UB2EVUFU]), SLEAN's cross-provider analyse → critique → arbitrate protocol, which rejected 47 of 69
AI-generated fixes as harmful (Vargas, 2025 [GAD5Z8PV]), unanimous LLM juries as an accept gate
(Ullah & Serwadda, 2026 [A6ZE2A26]), and MOSAICO's two-level architecture of solution and
supervision agents (Tisi et al., 2025 [DJMBHHZN]). A vision paper proposes an end-to-end agentic
review workflow with humans at quality gates (Kamalı et al., 2026 [3ZVMBGPB]).

The **reliability limits** of AI review are the better-evidenced half of this theme, and they form
an argument chain assembled by this review from independent sources:

1. **A model cannot reliably check its own output.** ChatGPT verifying its own work fails to report
   67% of its incorrect generations and 59% of its failed repairs, and 75% of the explanations in
   its self-generated test reports are inaccurate (Yu et al., 2024 [PPMTM4DG]). This is a lower
   bound measured in the most degenerate configuration (same session, own output in context), which
   is what makes it decisive against "have it review itself."
2. **Review without an external reference is circular.** Without an executable specification,
   generator and reviewer "reason from the same artefact, share the same training distribution, and
   exhibit correlated failures. The review checks code against itself, not against intent" (Zietsman,
   2026 [TA6GIUK2]). In planted-bug experiments, behaviour-driven specifications caught 5/5 while AI
   review ranged 0–100%, collapsing where domain knowledge was not inferable from the code; the
   author's own confound—"a docstring that encodes the convention is a specification"—suggests much
   informal evidence that AI review works may be spec-assisted without anyone noticing. Evidence is
   directional (single author, five functions per experiment); cite for the distinction, not the
   numbers.
3. **Supplying the reference produces a different failure, not no failure.** Given a natural-language
   requirement to check against, LLM reviewers systematically *overcorrect*—rejecting conformant
   code and inventing faults—with false-positive rates up to 88.74% for GPT-4o, and richer prompting
   makes it worse; a fix-guided verification filter that executes the proposed fix against tests
   cuts that to 39.96% (Jin & Chen, 2026 [UDVHQ5HR]). Overcorrection is the *expensive* failure for
   scalable oversight: it consumes the human attention the mechanism exists to conserve.
4. **The reviewer is attackable.** Crafted PR metadata re-introduced known CVEs past LLM security
   reviewers with 100% success; "attackers can iteratively refine attacks against a local clone of
   the review pipeline, while defenders have only one chance" (Mitropoulos et al., 2026 [X7EN6DXZ]).

**Deterministic checks (`rules-based-checks`, 22 studies; 13 under the governing `-v3` definition,
which covers static analysis, linters, scanners, type checkers, formal verifiers, and rule engines,
and excludes tests).** An external detector is worth more than a better prompt: feeding
static-analysis warnings back to Copilot Chat raised the security-fix rate from 19.3% unaided to
55.5%—nearly triple, and still leaving 44.5% unfixed (Fu et al., 2025 [U3IQJ4VK]). AIRA's 15
deterministic checks target code that "fails quietly," preserving the appearance of functionality
(Parris, 2026 [3SU9QZ6F]). VibeGuard gates five blind spots of AI-generated projects—artifact
hygiene, packaging drift, source-map exposure, hard-coded secrets, supply-chain risk—motivated by a
production source-map leak that existing scanners did not cover; 100% recall and 89.47% precision on
eight synthetic projects (Xie, 2026 [T8E8SCCG]). Töpfer et al. (2026 [72W6R4JG]) verify generated
adaptation managers against formal temporal constraints over execution traces and feed repairable
counterexamples to the model, with **no human code inspection** by design; fine-grained constraint
feedback converges in a few iterations where coarse metric feedback stalls. An AST-based
breaking-change detector supplies a computed, producer-independent triage signal by task type
(Ferdous et al., 2026 [UIXCRBQX]); a deletion-likelihood model (AUC 87.1%) predicts which generated
methods reviewers will remove (Watanabe et al., 2026 [E95T8E88]).

**Formal methods (`formal-methods`, 3 studies).** Annotates the technique, paired with whoever
performs it. Sistla et al. (2026 [5DI9B43K]) extract a formal representation of a code-reasoning
agent's answer and verify its reasoning steps with an external verifier—independence by
construction. Mitchell and Shaaban (2025 [6ZW9QNQH]) argue for a side-car that autoformalizes
specifications and feeds verification results back; no corpus paper delivers autoformalization plus
feedback (Töpfer's constraints are hand-authored), so the position stands as the corpus's statement
of that gap. The adoption barrier is the human effort to author specifications and proofs, not the
value of the guarantees; AI-automated formalization is the plausible uptake path, if it is cheap and
quiet enough to stay switched on.

#### 4.5.2 Triage — what reaches a human

**Risk routing (`risk-routing`, 17 studies; 4 primary) plus `routing-signal` (7 studies that supply
a signal without the allocation decision).** The contribution is the selection logic—which
artifacts reach a human, whether to escalate, at what autonomy tier—gated on a **computed,
producer-independent** signal. Meta trains diff-risk scores on historical severe faults and gates
diffs above a threshold, with graded gating from none to blocked (Abreu et al., 2025 [BU73N7PC]). A
creation-time "circuit breaker" predicts high-maintenance agent PRs from static cues with AUC 0.96,
capturing 69% of high-effort PRs at a 20% review budget (Minh et al., 2026 [74GE3TF7]). A
trust-calibrated multi-LLM pipeline propagates confidence across stages and routes between automated
gates and human review on model certainty, motivated by false-positive fatigue (Mahmud et al., 2025
[R9CDT9KB]). Hedwig adjusts a coding agent's autonomy across sessions from developer decisions,
loosening where trust is earned and tightening after corrections (Shukla et al., 2026 [T72TU8B5]).
Unanimous juries of strong models cut false accepts on SQL generation and are proposed as the
"what is safe to accept without a human" filter (Ullah & Serwadda, 2026 [A6ZE2A26]). Calibration
is the precondition: a well-calibrated confidence could drive graduated review intensity, but
intrinsic confidences are not calibrated (Spiess et al., 2025 [VTDG995V]).

The routing literature supplies far more **signals** than **allocation rules**: the review counts
seven studies that frame a signal for review attention without operationalizing selection, and the
production-evidenced routers are a handful.

#### 4.5.3 Fix — governing autonomous remediation

**Remediation gating (`remediation-gating`, 4 studies, 0 primary).** The thinnest stage. Governance
of fixes made *without* per-fix human involvement, by content gates (which fix candidates are
acceptable) and process gates (bounded retries, convergence rules, fail-closed termination). SLEAN
filters harmful fix suggestions through cross-critique and arbitration, reducing change surface
83–90% (Vargas, 2025 [GAD5Z8PV]); TheBotCompany's verification phase catches defects before
milestones close (Lyu et al., 2026 [UB2EVUFU]); Töpfer's loop aborts after ten unsuccessful
iterations (Töpfer et al., 2026 [72W6R4JG]); ZORO requires the agent to prove each rule was followed
before proceeding (Ma et al., 2026 [JCTP8VXP]). No corpus paper reports the distribution of
attempts-to-convergence or the fraction of loops that never converge.

#### 4.5.4 Escalate — the human's control surface and information

**HITL workflow (`hitl-workflow`, 33 studies, 10 primary)** is the design of the control point an
engaged human acts through: approval gates, checkpoints, action guards, bounded delegation. HULA,
deployed inside Atlassian Jira, lets engineers refine and approve coding plans and code at each
stage; engineers perceived reduced effort on straightforward tasks while quality concerns remained
(Takerngsaksiri et al., 2025 [5VTAJISY]). Shift-Up repurposes executable requirements (BDD),
architecture models (C4), and decision records as machine-readable guardrails that stabilize agent
behaviour and move human effort to design and validation (Lipsanen et al., 2026 [7SH86C2W]). ZORO
turns rules files from passive text into active, enforced controls (Ma et al., 2026 [JCTP8VXP]).
IACDM places external verification agents at discrete gates in an eight-phase process (Moreira, 2026
[RX9SICP9]). Professional developers "don't vibe, they control": they retain agency over design
and implementation out of insistence on quality attributes (Huang et al., 2025 [Z8TPRNEU]). The
corpus distinguishes two things the human does, and the review's own definitions keep them apart:
*evaluating* the result (judging what the AI produced) and *steering* (directing what it does
next); only the former is oversight.

**Oversight explanation (`oversight-explanation`, 27 studies, 7 primary)** is the information side:
helping the human understand what the AI is doing, by *push* (a decision-ready escalation handoff),
*pull* (visibility the human invokes), or *standing* explanations attached to output. Explanations of
how a patch was derived improved participants' judgement of patch correctness on five of six bugs,
and the tool signals when it is confident (Kang et al., 2024 [7UB2MD8Z]). Lumen shows developers the
dependency graph and summaries of the context the model will use (Goel & Melo, 2025 [VG6CIDQW]).
Casserini's intent-level telemetry exposes drift across tool calls and architectural boundaries
(Casserini et al., 2026 [95CPB7CF]). Heander et al. (2025 [F9JM9CI6]) argue for AI that *supports*
review rather than replacing it, preserving knowledge transfer and shared ownership.

A cluster of designs relocates the human's oversight object from the **artifact to a decision**.
Aporia makes design decisions "the shared medium between the programmer and the agent," elicited
proactively and traceable to code, and improved programmers' mental models of AI-written code
(Kasibatla et al., 2026 [ZH6QIU8A]); Scalable Interactive Oversight decomposes intent into a
recursive tree of low-burden decisions so non-experts produce expert-level requirements (Zhou et
al., 2026 [XRTVITVP]); HiLDE surfaces decision points during generation (González et al., 2025
[CI93QRUH], Context). The one **measurement** is a qualified negative: business professionals shown
natural-language explanations of AI-generated analyses, warned the AI makes mistakes, and prompted
to find them "frequently failed to detect critical flaws," many requiring no technical knowledge;
reformatting into delineated steps with alternatives per decision helped, and was not enough (Virk
& Liu, 2025 [22JBEZNK]). Widening the reviewer pool to non-developers is therefore not available as
a scaling lever on current interface designs—a boundary result the corpus has nowhere else.

#### 4.5.5 Cross-cutting — keeping the agent on mandate

`agent-scope-drift` (9 studies) marks mechanisms whose operand is the agent departing from what was
asked: CodeAgent's QA-Checker (Tang et al., 2024 [7V7SRG43]), Casserini's agentic entropy, Shift-Up's
drift reduction, and the practitioner observation that agents make unreviewed architectural
decisions. It is a distinct failure from producing buggy code: the code can be clean, tested, and
not what was asked for. Jin and Chen's conformance review (*did it build what was asked?*) is the
detection counterpart; a gate made entirely of defect review cannot see this failure because the
artifact is not defective.

### 4.6 SQ4 — How oversight is enacted

**Independence is the organizing property.** An oversight signal is informative only to the extent
it is produced independently of the artifact's producer. The corpus supplies all four legs of this
argument from separate sources: the design rule (routing must gate on a producer-independent
signal); the prescription (independent checkers and divergence detection: Zhu et al. [ZGST9CY6];
different models have "different blind spots": Mahmud et al. [R9CDT9KB]; an external formal
verifier: Sistla et al. [5DI9B43K]); the measurement (self-confidence is uncalibrated and
self-verification fails: Spiess [VTDG995V]; Yu [PPMTM4DG]); and the observed deviation (developers
delegate review back to the generator: Pimenova [E9RAWBDT]). Independence admits degrees, and the
corpus shows a gradient—same-model self-report < same-model rescaled against external labels <
different-vendor ensemble < external deterministic verifier—with practice sitting at the weakest
end.

Two refinements matter for design. **Decorrelation and reference are separate axes** (Zietsman
[TA6GIUK2]): a four-vendor panel with no specification is highly decorrelated and still has no
ground truth; it produces agreement, which can be mistaken for correctness. The panel papers
(Mahmud, Swidey, Zhu) move the decorrelation axis; the specification papers (Töpfer, Zhou
[XRTVITVP], Zietsman, Lipsanen) move the reference axis; they are complementary. And **the human
side has its own independence axis**: ordinary peer review has two degrees of separation (the
reviewer wrote neither the code nor the ticket). The default AI workflow—developer prompts,
developer accepts—has one, and an enterprise case in which a single senior expert sets constraints,
prompts, and performs the governance rollbacks has one (Bhatnagar, 2026 [P837LJWE], Context). An
organization can truthfully report that a human reviews all AI code while having *less* separation
than its pre-AI process.

**Multi-agent topologies.** "Multi-agent" in a title predicts nothing about architecture. The
corpus separates five shapes by what agents are asked and whether any can block:

| Shape | Agents answer | Aggregation | Failure mode | Instances |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Panel (redundant) | the same question | vote / consensus | correlated error survives unanimity | Ullah [A6ZE2A26] |
| Specialist gate-set (conjunctive) | different questions | all must pass; each holds a veto | false positives compound | — (design position) |
| Specialist advisory | different questions | report, no veto | gaps between specialisms | Tisi [DJMBHHZN]; Kamalı [3ZVMBGPB] |
| Relay | different questions | sequential; each consumes the prior's output | errors amplify | Rasheed [DJHG9BBS], Context **[verify key]** |
| **Precision filter** | validates the prior stage's output | admit / suppress | **errors damped — the only measured mitigation** | Sun [V4IRKSFI]; Jin [UDVHQ5HR] |

**Redundancy has three uses, and only one fails.** As a *quality signal* (N agreed, therefore
correct) it fails: consensus selection among code-generation ensembles filters out minority-correct
solutions and amplifies shared error (Vallecillos-Ruiz et al., 2025 [FRV9ZXRW], Context);
agreement between two vendors improved fix acceptance by only 2.4 points (Vargas [GAD5Z8PV]);
self-organizing LLM teams fall up to 37.6% short of their best member by averaging expert and
non-expert views (Pappu et al., 2026 [2XV8ZVM8], Context); and this review's own panel agreed 9/9
on wrong tags (§3.9). As a *generator* of candidate findings it works—SGCR's ensemble exists for
"exploration of diverse" suggestions, with a second-order aggregator deciding. As *adjudication*—a
distinct stage asking "is this finding valid?"—it works and is the only measured false-positive
reduction in the corpus (Sun: 75% precision in production; Jin: 88.74% → 39.96%). The adjudicator is
a **shared** stage: BitsAI-CR runs 219 rules through one checker and one filter, so cost is N + 1,
not 2N.

**What a second checker contributes is missed requirements, not missed defects.** Rubric failures
in agentic verifiers classify as "Root Cause Missed" (17.5%) and "Missing Edges" (15.1%) even when
tests pass (Raghavendra et al., 2026 [8VBH957K], Context); conformance review is a distinct axis
from defect review (Jin). The value of a second checker is coverage of the specification space,
which also explains why deterministic checks and LLM review are complements: a linter cannot notice
an absent requirement.

**Specification validation is the leverage point.** A state machine guarantees that verification
*happens*; it is silent on whether verification is against the *right criterion*. Shi et al. (2026
[MFSZPSPU]) have an LLM draft a per-bug rubric, a human review it once, and the LLM apply it to
arbitrarily many patches (κ 0.75, recall 0.94, precision 0.80)—the only structure in the corpus
whose human cost is O(1) in the criteria rather than O(N) in the outputs. SGCR grounds in
human-authored specifications; Lipsanen makes the specification executable; Zietsman makes it the
gate. Composed, they give the strongest design the corpus supports: *specification
(human-validated) → execution → verification against the validated specification, by deterministic
means where possible*. No single paper has all three, and only one reports validating its criteria.

**Where the gate sits.** Ordering the mechanisms by how early they act reveals a spectrum no single
paper names: before the model exists (curate training data and evaluate models on security: Ji et
al., 2024 [YA7XNWYE]); before the artifact exists (self-throttling when project debt is high,
proposed by He et al. [REZGA5WF]); before the work arrives (intake restriction: Yang [XJAXB98T]);
before the artifact is specified (decision elicitation and formal constraints: Kasibatla, Zhou,
Töpfer); between generation and merge (nearly everything); after merge (debt measurement). The
field's attention concentrates at the position where each artifact must be handled individually by
a human whose attention is the scarce resource; the upstream positions are structurally cheaper and
occupied by one paper each.

**The latency window.** Mechanisms also sort by the time between generation and consequence. Where
that window is zero—LLM-generated code executed the moment it is produced—only containment is
possible (Shinde et al., 2026 [QWHE9EXH], Context). Where it is short (CI, agentic PRs), automated
gates and routing apply; where it is long (specification, architecture), human review and debate
do. Applying a long-window mechanism to a zero-window problem yields oversight theatre; the
dissertation's scope is the reviewable window.

### 4.7 SQ5 — How oversight scales

**Only one gate scales.** Risk routing operates at two levels. *Gate 1 (access)* asks whether an
artifact needs human review at all and reduces the count of human touches; *gate 2 (depth)* asks how
much, given that it does, and reallocates a fixed workload. All scaling leverage lives at gate 1; a
system with no gate 1 has human-attention cost growing linearly with output however good its
prioritization. Most routing papers are gate-1 designs (Abreu, Minh, Mahmud, Zhu's risk-weighted
sampling). The corpus's one gate-2-only design—adversarial verification requiring human review of
everything in its domain and varying only depth (Swidey, 2026 [5RLPIA3K])—inherits its
classification from the EU AI Act's Annex III, which declares the domain high-risk before the system
sees anything; given a classifier that returns "high" for everything, 100% review is the correct
policy, and the absence of scaling is inherited, not chosen. If scaling lives at gate 1, the quality
of the risk assessment is the whole game.

**Gate 1 is reached from opposite directions.** *Load-reducing* routing asks what can safely skip
review (human load down); *coverage-ensuring* routing asks what must not skip it (load up; e.g.,
detecting AI-authored code to target it for review). Only the first is a scaling mechanism; the
second is a quality mechanism using the same machinery. A count of routing papers cannot be read as
a count of scaling mechanisms without separating them, and an organization adopting both is pulling
in two directions.

**Both directions share a failure mode: the un-routed remainder.** The absence of a routing signal
is read as a clean bill of health. In a token-level uncertainty-highlighting study, participants
"interpreted a lack of highlights as signal that the code was correct," and the authors caution
that they may be "simply shifting the automation bias" (Vasconcelos et al. [ZBF86IJM], Context;
assistive, n = 30, accuracy effect not significant). Promotion to a finding awaits an instance at
artifact granularity, but the concern is structural to routing as such.

**Quality scaling is not throughput scaling** (§2.2). McAleese, Kang, and Zhou demonstrate the
first; none demonstrates the second. The genuinely throughput-relevant finding in McAleese is that
critics found "hundreds of errors in ChatGPT training data rated as flawless"—coverage that human
review at volume had already lost.

**What scales, on the corpus's evidence.** Three structures change the economics rather than
reallocate a budget. (1) *Load-reducing routing on a computed signal*: Minh's 20% budget capturing
69% of high-effort PRs; Abreu's gating of Y% of faults by gating X% of diffs with Y ≫ X. (2)
*Validate the criterion once, apply it N times*: Shi's O(1) human cost. (3) *Certify upstream*:
human-certified module repositories let every downstream use inherit vetting performed once (Enyedi
[VCI88UZD], Context), the economics the agent-skills marketplace lacks—26.1% of 31,132 scanned
skills carry a vulnerability (Liu et al. [6ZC3H7AF], Context). A fourth, from design context rather
than evidence, is *bounded retry*: a fixed retry budget converts an unbounded convergence problem
into a detected deadlock and makes human load predictable (escalations = deadlock rate × volume);
the optimal budget depends on a distribution no corpus paper reports.

**The authoring-side limit.** Swapping in an agent reviewer does not close the loop when the
bottleneck is the coding agent's capacity to iterate under feedback: small PRs that look safe
"stall because the agent cannot handle subjective feedback," a *silent abandonment* that a
structural router (AUC 0.958) misses as false negatives (Minh et al., 2026 [74GE3TF7]). The corpus
measures agents as producers and as reviewers; it does not measure them as negotiators.

### 4.8 SQ6 — Governance, audit, and evidence

**Organizational governance (`org-governance`, 20 studies, 8 primary).** The observed practice is
Yang et al.'s twelve strategies in open source (§4.4). The one enterprise case is a governance-first
blueprint—usage policy, content filtering, security scanning, human oversight—deployed at a global
e-commerce company with a 20× increase in deployment frequency and no loss of reliability
(Karuppuchamy, 2026 [8MXATG38]; single-organization self-report). Frameworks include a risk
assessment that classifies LLM use by autonomy × impact to set oversight levels (Otten et al., 2026
[ZUM76CCG]); a hybrid-team operating model with tiered autonomy and quantified transition criteria
(Bara, 2026 [6F3S8IB7]); a licensing-risk governance framework for AI-generated code (Sharma et al.,
2025 [WPWF7A32]); an expert-validated model of cybersecurity risks and mitigations for generative
code (Al-Hashimi, 2026 [6DXZGHD9]); and Zhu et al.'s catalogue of oversight patterns with signals to
track (Zhu et al., 2026 [ZGST9CY6]). A roadmap for interaction-time governance asks that prompts,
retrieval scope, and checks be reviewable and replayable (Migliarini et al., 2026 [4AXDVW7J]).
Practitioner guidance from industry authors supplies background and anecdote rather than evidence
(Kim & Yegge, 2025 [RPHK78A9]).

**Regulatory compliance (`regulatory-compliance`, 10 studies, 3 primary).** Three papers
operationalize an external obligation into engineering controls: NIST AI RMF mapped to lifecycle
controls and evidence artifacts for critical infrastructure, validated in a 16-week energy-sector
deployment and expert-reviewed (Sudarsan et al., 2025 [UW2R6BBJ]); EU AI Act Article 14 mapped
onto an adversarial-verification architecture for employment AI (Swidey, 2026 [5RLPIA3K]; a vendor
white paper, to be cited with that caveat); and quantified ethical and regulatory risk indices for
AI-assisted banking software (Vanam et al., 2025 [R4WJZBSF]; design-only, with a mock
demonstration). The corpus holds two readings of Article 14 that are both right because they answer
different questions: the permissive reading (the Act mandates oversight *capability*, not per-item
review) concerns what it requires in general; the conservative reading (per-item human
interpretation) concerns what it requires once a system is inside Annex III. The disagreement is
over the scope of the high-risk class—exactly the gate-1/gate-2 split. The scaling claim is also
reached from the regulatory side: the compliance obligation cannot be discharged at the volumes
automation produces (Tuape et al., 2025 [XZEHQYNZ], Context). Article 14(4)(a)'s requirement that
the overseer remain aware of the tendency to over-rely makes automation bias a compliance concern,
not only a quality one.

**Provenance and auditability (`provenance-auditability`, 19 studies, 1 primary).** The need is
stated most sharply by Wang et al. (2026 [2KPHQ5IV]): vibe coding leaves no record of the structural
commitments behind the code, so they propose a typed property graph as the primary artifact from
which code is derived, with evidence linked to structural claims. Shift-Up's decision records and
executable requirements restore traceability (Lipsanen); Migliarini's replayable interaction-time
controls record what policy and context were bound at invocation. Most of the 19 studies carry the
theme as a secondary membership: audit trails are a component of many designs and the contribution
of few.

**Tooling supply chain (`tooling-supply-chain`, 5 studies).** Attacks on the reviewer are
supply-chain attacks on the oversight layer (Mitropoulos [X7EN6DXZ]); a unified security testing
architecture for autonomous coding agents argues SAST and DAST assumptions no longer hold (Hjazeen,
2026 [VFNJSZD9]); a policy report locates risk at the model layer and argues for shifting
responsibility to the organizations best positioned to reduce systemic risk (Ji et al., 2024
[YA7XNWYE]).

### 4.9 Risk × mitigation matrix

The risk-type facets record substantive engagement with a harm; the matching themes record that the
paper does the empirical or mechanism lift. Reading them together:

| Harm | Studies engaging it | …carrying ≥1 solution theme | …lifting the matching problem theme |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Quality / debt (`risk-quality`) | 47 | 41 | 17 |
| Over-reliance (`risk-overreliance`) | 31 | 29 | 15 |
| Security (`risk-security`) | 26 | 23 | 9 |
| IP / licensing (`risk-ip`) | 5 | 5 | — |
| Bias in generated code (`risk-bias`) | 4 | 4 | — |

Quality and security are both identified and mitigated at scale; over-reliance is widely identified
and mitigated mainly by interface design; IP/licensing and bias are identified by a handful of
studies, with the licensing response confined to a single governance framework and no measurement
of either in the wild. Commitment and forecast—how much work is promised, in what order, against
what capacity—are governed by no mechanism in the corpus; every gate reviewed governs the artifact,
none the intake.

### 4.10 Methodological findings from the multi-model process

Reported because they bear on the dissertation's own thesis and on any reader who intends to use
language models in a review.

- **Model agreement is not validity.** At screening, models agreed with one another (κ 0.49–0.64) far
  more than with the human (κ ≤ 0.30); their dissent did not predict the human; and at coding,
  three vendors agreed 9/9 on tags the human rejected because they shared a misreading of the
  instrument. Decorrelating the model does not decorrelate the prompt.
- **The panel is a recall instrument, not a judge.** It recalled 91.6% of blind human tags and the
  human overturned 9.4% of its modal proposals on surviving papers; it flagged only 57.4% of the
  studies the human later demoted. Tag recall and tier recall are different numbers.
- **Abstract-level inclusion over-includes by half.** 51% of abstract-level Cores were demoted at
  full text, all in one direction.
- **Human protocol drifts toward rigour under use.** The arbiter's pass tightened from
  challenge-the-doubtful to confirm-or-reject-every-tag; silence acquired three meanings and had to
  be closed out explicitly.
- **The pipeline exhibited the corpus's failure modes.** A machine stage overturned human decisions
  without review; a counter mistook one rater's two tags for two raters; silence was read as
  confirmation. Each was caught by audit, none by the pipeline.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 What the corpus establishes

The review question asked how organizations practice and scale human oversight of AI-generated
code. The honest summary is that the literature answers the *practice* half from open-source
repositories, answers the *scale* half mostly with proposals, and barely measures whether any of it
works.

**The problem is established.** Both halves of the inversion are measured in production
repositories by independent groups: AI-generated code is riskier per unit (Fu; Liu; He; Parris) and
receives less inspection (Gao; Branco), with the prescription for more scrutiny (He) and the
observed deviation from it (Gao) coming from studies that do not cite each other. The inversion is
not universal but bimodal, and governance maturity predicts which mode a repository is in. That
turns the dissertation's question from *whether* to *in which organizations and why*.

**Six failure mechanisms, with different remedies.** The corpus distinguishes skipped review,
misallocated attention, substitution of senior building capacity, intake restriction, reviewability
decay, and engagement decay. An organization observing "oversight is strained" could be in any of
them. Adding review capacity helps the first; the third means capacity is already being paid for
from a budget nobody tracks; the fifth is not helped by capacity at all; the sixth makes oversight
quality a function of time on task, which no organizational control in the corpus accounts for.

**Three structural results about checks.** A model's assessment of its own output is not a check
(Yu; Spiess). Agreement between models is not validation (Vallecillos-Ruiz; Vargas; Pappu; this
review's own panel). Supplying an external reference is necessary and produces a second failure
mode—overcorrection—so that the review task is two-sided and fixing one error direction moves cost
into the other (Zietsman; Jin). These are structural: they do not improve as models improve, because
they concern independence and reference, not capability. The chain *self-check fails → therefore
independent, grounded checking* is assembled by this review from papers that do not cite one
another; it is labelled as synthesis, and its positive half rests on Mahmud's observation that
different models have different blind spots, which is routing on disagreement rather than a
head-to-head test.

**Where the field's effort is, and where leverage is.** Detection between generation and merge is
densely populated, well built (31 built systems), and occasionally deployed (six). Triage has many
signals and few allocation rules; remediation gating has four studies; upstream positions
(specification validation, intake, model-layer responsibility) have one paper each. Only
load-reducing routing on a computed, producer-independent signal changes the economics of human
attention; validating the criterion once (Shi) and certifying upstream (Enyedi) change them
differently. Everything else reallocates a fixed budget.

**The literature is rich in deciding where to look and poor in measuring whether looking works.**
Criterion-setting—routing, prioritization, allocation—is among the largest clusters. Sensitivity
measurement—whether the reviewer catches what is shown—is nearly absent: Eze operationalizes it as
override rate, Zhu prescribes signals to track, Langer supplies the formalism, and McKay names the
measurement gap outright. This may partly be an artifact of an instrument built to capture
mechanisms rather than outcomes **[verify at F5 gap assessment]**, but the thinness of
effectiveness evidence is also the finding that justifies the organizational survey: practitioners
have operating experience the literature does not.

### 5.2 Named gaps

The following are stated as gaps the corpus does not fill, each a candidate contribution for the
dissertation or for a small, cheap study.

1. **Same-model vs different-model checking, on the same artifacts.** No study compares *A generates
   → A checks* against *A generates → B checks*. Yu's published setup would support it.
2. **Correlation between specialist reviewers.** Whether independent-looking agents share errors
   decides whether a conjunctive gate compounds false positives or buys no redundancy. Unmeasured.
3. **Attempts-to-convergence.** Every multi-agent paper reports end-to-end outcomes; none reports
   how many rounds convergence took or what fraction never converged—the number a retry budget needs.
4. **Requirement coverage as the metric.** If a second checker's value is missed requirements, the
   right measure is coverage of the specification space; every paper measures defect precision.
5. **Suppressed findings.** Precision filters remove findings, and what they remove is invisible to
   precision and adoption metrics alike. No study measures the false-negative cost of suppression.
6. **Autoformalization plus feedback.** Advocated (Mitchell), half-built (Töpfer, with hand-authored
   constraints), never delivered.
7. **Error compounding across relays.** Whether a pipeline of reviewing agents corrects or amplifies
   upstream mistakes is unaddressed, and the relay is the common production shape.
8. **Commitment and forecast.** No mechanism governs how much work is promised or sequenced against
   capacity; every gate governs merges, none governs intake.
9. **Disciplines other than code.** Agentic delivery produces requirements, test plans, architecture
   decisions, infrastructure code, runbooks, and documentation; each has a different reviewer and
   failure mode, and the corpus governs only source artifacts.
10. **An outcome measure of oversight effectiveness.** Determinants (bias, theatre, evaluator
    reliability) are well described; whether oversight *caught anything* has no home.
11. **Bimodality outside open source.** Whether the all-or-none auto-merge pattern survives where
    reviewers are employees who cannot abandon a PR is untested.
12. **Review quality across a session.** Engagement decay predicts measurable degradation from the
    first to the last change reviewed; no corpus paper tests it and an organization could.
13. **The organizational-level decision.** Whether organizations bifurcate governance between
    assistive and agentic use, or apply one policy to both—routing at the level of modes rather than
    artifacts—is asked by no study.

### 5.3 Implications for the dissertation's survey

The corpus converts directly into survey constructs, and the review's instrument design document
holds a 55-item starter bank with provenance for each item. The constructs the evidence most clearly
supports asking about are:

- **Routing and its signal.** What lets a change skip review and, separately, what forces one into
  review; what *produces* that signal (the assistant itself, a different model, a deterministic
  tool), and whether the choice was deliberate. The expected answer—the assistant, unexamined—is the
  weakest configuration on the independence gradient.
- **Independence on the human side.** Whether the person who reviews AI output is the one who
  prompted it, and what else in the loop is independent.
- **External reference.** Whether AI output is checked against a specification, tests, or
  constraints, or only by other models; whether tests are written by the tool that wrote the code.
- **The inspection point.** Whether there is a point where a human is required to read the code,
  and if not, what replaced it—since zero-inspection arrives both by design (Töpfer) and by drift
  (Waseem), and the two look identical in the artifact.
- **Validation of AI findings.** Whether anything filters or ranks AI review findings before a
  human sees them; whether anyone has turned a check off for noise; whether anything is measured
  about what the checker misses.
- **Criteria.** Where the reviewer's criteria came from, whether a document exists, who reviewed
  it, and when it last changed—because criteria quality is decisive and one paper validates them.
- **Mechanism of strain.** Which of the six failure mechanisms an organization is in, probed through
  observable events: senior time reviewing vs authoring; intake rules; whether reviewers can
  determine what the AI assumed; whether review quality differs across a session.
- **Escalation.** Retry budgets, triggers, and what happens to work that exhausts them.

Design cautions follow from the corpus too. Ask about artifacts and events, not attitudes or
effort: developer actions are shaped by release pressure and ownership boundaries, not comment
quality (Karakaya et al., 2026 [5NZ2EDEK], Context). Do not ask by architecture name: five
topologies share the label "multi-agent." Expect the honest answer to many items to be "nothing" or
"we don't know," and treat that as the finding.

### 5.4 Implications for practice

Stated cautiously, since the evidence base is young. An organization that must choose where to
spend scarce review attention has corpus support for: gating on a signal computed independently of
the generator rather than on the tool's confidence; adding a validation stage between AI reviewers
and developers, since noisy advice is ignored and noisy gates stop delivery; keeping panels for what
they are good at (surfacing missed requirements) and not using agreement as the decision; investing
in the criteria that checks run against, where one human review is amortized over every artifact;
and distinguishing, for its own process, whether review is being skipped, misallocated, substituted,
refused, defeated by the artifact, or eroded over a session—because the remedies differ.

### 5.5 Alternative views and counterpoints

The review recorded contrary positions as it met them, to guard against confirmation bias. Review
can *reduce* revision of AI output by converting accountability into deference (Zhou & Zhao
[E689ZAXC]). Policies requiring human oversight of algorithms may assume a reliability of human
correction that the evidence does not support (Green [9HPPSFM6], Context **[verify; unread at
closeout]**). Assurance filters can make AI code no more burdensome to review than human code and
place the human outside the loop entirely (Alshahwan et al. [FZK2QB5A], Context); validation by
execution can be *more* trustworthy for having no human in it, within the defect classes an
exploit can demonstrate (David & Gervais [6NTZ85CW], Context). Developers recruited to review hold AI
output to teammate standards (Omidvar-Tehrani [4FGIVVTG]). Agents introduce fewer breaking changes
than humans (Ferdous [UIXCRBQX]). And the inspection point can be removed deliberately, with a
formal checker standing in for it (Töpfer [72W6R4JG])—which relocates the human to the
specification, where nothing checks the specification. Each of these qualifies the thesis without
overturning it, and each generates a survey item.

## 6. Limitations

**Of the corpus.** Sixty-three of 72 included studies are from 2025–2026 and 32 are preprints; the
field will have moved before this review is read, and some findings will have been revised in
publication. Much of the problem-side evidence is repository mining of open source, where
maintainers can refuse work in ways employed reviewers cannot, so the inversion's organizational
dynamics are extrapolated. Many solution studies are designs (15) or evaluated on synthetic data
(11); six report adoption outside a research setting. Thirty of 72 studies produce no empirical
evidence of their own. Several high-value results rest on small or directional studies (Catalan, n
= 4; Omidvar-Tehrani, n = 11; Zietsman, five functions per experiment) and are cited for mechanism
and shape, not magnitude. One included source is a vendor white paper and one a practitioner book;
both are cited as framing with that caveat.

**Of the method.** A single arbiter made every tier and tag decision, assisted by a language model
whose checks are correlated with the arbiter's; the mitigation is a binding public record of every
ruling, never edited in place. The citable blinded Pass-2 κ was planned and not run. The instrument
moved during the blind calibration round. The sub-questions were refined after extraction. Sixty-
seven records from one arXiv batch remain unscreened by decision. Google Scholar citation counts
for the included studies, planned as an authority signal independent of venue, have not yet been
retrieved **[E8, pending]**, and the corpus-wide gap assessment that would confirm or retire several
candidate silences above has not yet been run **[F5, pending]**.

**Of the use of language models.** Models screened and coded at volume; the human reviewed every
record above a relevance floor and every tag that entered synthesis, but records below the floor
were excluded without human review. The pipeline's own failures—a machine stage reversing human
decisions, a vote counter mistaking tags for raters, silence read as confirmation—were found by
audit rather than prevented, and are reported in §3.10 and §4.10.

---

## 7. Conclusion

AI coding tools produce code faster than human review can inspect it, and the code is riskier per
unit than what it replaces. This review found the inversion measured on both sides, found six
distinct ways in which oversight gives way under volume, and found a solution literature that is
dense at detection, thin at triage and remediation, and nearly silent on whether any of it works.
Three results hold regardless of model capability: a producer cannot check itself; agreement is not
validation; and a reference makes checking possible without making it reliable. The one mechanism
that scales human attention—letting some artifacts skip human review on a computed, independent
signal—depends entirely on the quality of that signal, and the one place where a single human
judgement is amortized over many artifacts is the validation of the criteria the checks run against.

Organizations are making these decisions now, without a baseline of what comparable organizations
have tried or found effective. That is the gap the dissertation's survey and interviews address.
The review supplies its constructs, its cautions, and its reason to expect that the most common
answer—"a human reviews everything"—will turn out to describe less oversight than the process it
replaced.

## Appendix B. The thematic instrument (v2.13 frozen vocabulary, with F2 additions)

**Problem family.** `oversight-scaling-inversion` (AI code riskier yet less inspected; the gating act or its absence; `-v2` re-specified around a leakage test) · `ai-code-insecurity` (empirical security weakness evidence) · `quality-debt` (non-security degradation: debt, complexity, smells, comprehensibility).

**Limits family.** `automation-bias` (a *capable* human fails at oversight) · `oversight-theater` (review exists without authority, time, or information to change the outcome) · `evaluator-reliability` (reliability of an *automated* evaluator; added post-freeze).

**Detect.** `ai-review` (probabilistic detector: an LLM judges; carries the reliability limits of agent-checks-agent) · `rules-based-checks` (deterministic detector; `-v3`: static analysis, scanners, type checkers, verifiers, rule engines; tests excluded) · `formal-methods` (technique tag, composed with the performer).

**Triage.** `risk-routing` (selection/escalation logic on a computed, producer-independent signal; signal-only contributions carry the `routing-signal` facet instead).

**Fix.** `remediation-gating` (governance of autonomous fixing: content and process gates).

**Escalate.** `hitl-workflow` (the human's control surface: gates, checkpoints, bounded delegation) · `oversight-explanation` (helping the human understand: push, pull, standing).

**Cross-cutting.** `agent-scope-drift` (mechanisms whose operand is the agent departing from intent).

**Governance.** `org-governance` (the organization's own apparatus) · `regulatory-compliance` (external mandate operationalized).

**Supply chain and provenance.** `tooling-supply-chain` (vetting of AI tooling; attacks on the reviewer) · `provenance-auditability` (persistent, auditable record of AI changes).

**Facets.** Role: `problem-statement-anchor`, `survey-input`(`-v2`), `intro-framing`, `lit-review`, `counterpoint` (deprecated). Form/maturity: `design-only`, `built-system`, `adopted`, `expert-validated`, `framework`, `evaluated-synthetic`, `evaluated-benchmark`, `evaluated-real-data`. Scope: `general-ai`, `general-code`, `non-developer`. Mode: `assistive`, `agentic`. Contribution: `steering`, `metrics`, `routing-signal`, `deterministic-orchestration`(`-v2`), `agent-panel`, `peer-critique`, `cross-model`. Risk type: `risk-security`, `risk-quality`, `risk-overreliance`, `risk-ip`, `risk-bias`. Method: `method-self-report`, `method-mining`, `method-experiment`, `method-field-study`.

Full definitions, boundaries, and worked examples: `slr-phase4/Tag_Prompt_v2.13.md` (instrument of record) and `slr-phase4/Taxonomy_Changelog.md` (every ruling).
